Upsells — bought in the moment

Added after the entry, when the golfer has already paid and is standing over the ball. Jackpot upgrade, double-up, three-for-two. They attach to entries already counted — they never create new volume.

Why the cover works

Investors want recurring revenue. An insurer cannot cover unlimited swings for a fixed premium. Both are satisfied by capping the allowance — four swings a month is a known exposure, so it can be priced; everything above it is bought on the tee, where the premium arrives with the risk.

4 included swings at $500 — expected claim$0.16
Premium ceded on the membership (5%)$0.50
Trade-up — premium ceded (24% of $17 avg)$4.08
Trade-up — expected claim$1.30
Blended loss ratio31.9%
Aces must run this much more often before cover loses money2.51×

The same 31.9% as pay-per-play alone — the included swings are priced at the same target, so adding a subscription is neutral to the insurer. For contrast: unlimited swings at the $10,000 tier would be $12.80 of expected claim against $2.40 of premium — a 533% loss ratio. That is the version no one will write, and the version this replaces.

The full underwriting submission

Five tiers from $1 to $100, a flat 1-in-12,500 per-attempt probability, 100% cover and 20% brokerage to Indwe. Stress-tested for ace frequency, for adverse selection into the top three tiers, and for a full $100,000 claim landing in year one — the book stays in profit through all of them. The workbook is in the dataroom. No revised policy is bound on these terms as at August 2026.

Straight about the risk

The three questions we get asked first

Each one has an answer, and the answer is in the numbers rather than in the adjectives. The full risk register — including the ones without a clean answer yet — is in the dataroom.

What if Santam walks?

They sponsor R9m over three years and carry 100% of prize risk. Sponsorship is R2.5m of our R4.0m trailing revenue. If they did not renew we would lose that income and have to re-place the underwriting.

The answer

The prize-indemnity market has 20+ specialist carriers globally, and on the submitted terms this is a profitable book — a 31.9% loss ratio with 2.51× headroom. Re-placing it would cost us margin and time, not the business. We are targeting a second carrier on the panel before the global launch. The plan already holds SA sponsorship flat at R3.5m after the contract expires in 2028.

What if no simulator operator signs?

R12.0m of the R43.3m 2029 plan — about 20% — depends on an agreement that does not exist. No approach has been made to any operator as at August 2026.

The answer

Ernie holds direct relationships with the operators on the target list, and R1.6m of this round makes us technically ready to sign rather than ready to pitch. Neither is a contract. Strip the simulator line out entirely and 2029 is R31.3m, the milestone R93.9m and your return 1.98× rather than 2.76×. We would rather you underwrite that number and be surprised.

What if a regulator calls it gambling?

We operate as a skill competition with no gaming licence. That is our position in South Africa and it is untested in the markets we plan to enter.

The answer

A written opinion on the South African position is in the dataroom. No market entry happens without local counsel first, and both the app and the simulator product can be geo-fenced per jurisdiction at the platform level. The product is structured so that skill, not chance, determines the outcome — there is no wagering and no odds-based payout anywhere in it.

And the one we get asked about most

What if someone actually wins? The insurer pays, and it is the best marketing day of the year. Every prize is pre-funded and 100% underwritten. The book is stress-tested for aces running 2.5× more often than amateur odds predict, for low-handicap players selecting into the top tiers, and for a full $100,000 claim landing in year one. It stays in profit through all three.

Straight answers

Investor FAQ

Is this gambling?

No. It's a purely skill-based competition — no randomness, no wagering, no odds-based payouts — so it sits outside gambling regulation and needs no gaming licence. A written legal opinion on the South African position is in the dataroom. Every prize is fully insured and pre-funded.

What if someone actually wins R1,000,000?

The insurer pays. Every prize is 100% underwritten and verified by dual cameras plus an AI shot tracer. On the submitted terms the premium is $4.08 an entry against an expected claim of $1.30 — a 31.9% loss ratio, with aces needing to run 2.51× more often than amateur odds predict before the cover stops making money. A big winner is the best marketing day of the year.

How do the course partnerships work?

Courses earn 10% of every entry with zero upfront cost, on exclusive two-year auto-renewing agreements with restraint of trade; we cover installation, insurance, tech and ops. Metropolitan and Boschenmeer already run two holes each.

Who else does this? What's the moat?

A world first; the only meaningful local competitor was acquired and rebranded into Get Lucky. The moat: exclusive locked-in course contracts, owned verification tech, a first-party platform, and — slowest of all to replicate — a live relationship with a national underwriter. Compounded by Ernie's name.

How does the money actually work?

Three parts. Membership — R149/month locally, $10/month globally — buys access and carries no prize risk. The entry buys one swing at one insured prize, and 24% of it is ceded as premium on that same swing. Upsells are added in the moment, on top of an entry. We sell that swing in three places: installed courses, the global app and simulators. Reported net of the insurer, course and operator shares, so the streams sit on one basis and can be added.

What exactly am I buying in this round?

17% of Get Lucky Golf Club (Pty) Ltd for R8m at a R47m post-money valuation (R39m pre), alongside the founders and founding investor Ernie Els. Minimum tickets from R1m. Full terms, the cap table and the valuation model are in the dataroom.

How real is the simulator business?

The market is already built — we're monetising demand, not creating it. Simulators are a $2.4bn global market; South Korea alone logs ~94M rounds a year across ~9,000 venues, and Golfzon runs 6,500+ of them. Those machines already measure every shot, so there's no hardware and no installation on our side. What the venues lack is something to play for — an insured prize and the underwriting behind it, which is the slow part to build and the hard part to copy. Ernie opens those doors himself. R1.6m of this round builds the integration; the plan then takes 1.5% of one country's rounds by 2029 and counts no other market. Operator agreements are not yet signed; the dataroom sets out the adoption range.
